You don’t have to be brave. You have to be willing.

There’s a myth that supporting a movement means risking everything you have. It doesn’t. The overwhelming majority of this work is ordinary, lawful, and badly needed — and it looks a lot more like a church disaster-relief committee than anything you’ve seen on the news.

Here’s the work:

Keep people safe. Stock first aid supplies, eyewash, saline nasal mist, gloves. Pack kits. Organize medical supplies so they’re ready before they’re needed.

Keep people warm. Winter gear drives. Blankets and hand warmers. A heated room to rotate through when it’s twenty below in Butte.

Keep people fed. Cook. Run the meal train. Make sure nobody puts in a long day on an empty stomach.

Keep people accounted for. Legal observing. Phone trees. Rides when someone needs one. Making sure everyone who showed up gets home.

Keep people company. Waiting with someone until they’re released. Carrying a message to someone who needs to hear from the outside. Being a steady phone number on a hard day.

Keep people ready. De-escalation training. Know Your Rights workshops. Preparation, so that nothing happens by accident.

Keep the lights on. Fiscal sponsorship. Bail funds. The unglamorous financial plumbing that makes everything above it possible.

None of this is a gray area. Most of it is published in guides written by lawyers, specifically so community groups can do it lawfully.

You can give an hour or a Saturday. You can give a casserole or a car. Find the one that fits your life.
